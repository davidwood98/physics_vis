Limits and fits, explained

What an IT grade is

No part comes out exactly to size, so a drawing gives a tolerance: how far the real size may vary. ISO 286 standardises tolerances as international tolerance grades, IT01 to IT18. A lower number is tighter. The width of a grade also grows with size, because big parts are harder to make accurately: an IT7 tolerance is 12 µm wide at 6 mm but 46 µm at 250 mm. Sizes are grouped into ranges such as "over 18 up to and including 30 mm", and every size in a range shares the same values.

The older edition of the standard (via Engineers Edge) gives the formula behind grades IT5 to IT18: a tolerance factor i = 0.45 ∛D + 0.001 D micrometres, with D the geometric mean of the size range in millimetres, multiplied by 7 for IT5, 10 for IT6, 16 for IT7 and so on up to 100 for IT11. The published table values are rounded, so the table, not the formula, is what you use.

Fundamental deviation letters

The grade says how wide the tolerance zone is; a letter says where it sits relative to the nominal size. Capital letters are for holes, lower case for shafts. Shafts lettered c, d, e, f and g sit below the nominal size, by a fixed amount called the fundamental deviation, so they are always smaller than the hole. An h shaft touches the nominal size from below. js straddles it equally (±IT/2). k, m, n, p, r, s, t and u sit above it by increasing amounts, so the shaft starts to overlap the hole. For a few letters the standard splits a size range in two, for example u uses different values over 18 up to 24 mm and over 24 up to 30 mm.

The hole-basis system, and why it is preferred

In the hole-basis system every hole is H: its smallest size is exactly the nominal size and it can only be larger. The fit is chosen by choosing the shaft. That suits the workshop: holes are made with fixed-size tools such as drills and reamers and checked with fixed-size plug gauges, while a shaft can be turned or ground to any diameter. One reamer and one gauge for each hole size then serve every kind of fit (RoyMech puts it as keeping the standard hole-making tools and processes). Machinery's Handbook adds that a shaft-basis system (h shafts, holes chosen to suit) is the one to use when a common shaft mates with several holes.

How to read "25 H7/g6"

25 is the nominal size in millimetres. H7 is the hole: letter H, grade IT7. g6 is the shaft: letter g, grade IT6. Hole first, then shaft. Where the hole's zone sits wholly above the shaft's, there is always a gap (a clearance fit). Where it sits wholly below, the parts always overlap and must be pressed or shrunk together (an interference fit). Where the zones overlap, a pair might be either (a transition fit).

Worked example: 25 H7/g6

At 25 mm the 18 to 30 mm range applies. IT7 is 21 µm and IT6 is 13 µm. The hole is H, so it runs from 25.000 to 25.021 mm. The fundamental deviation of g at this size is −7 µm, so the shaft runs from 25 − 0.007 − 0.013 = 24.980 mm up to 24.993 mm. The loosest pair, the biggest hole with the smallest shaft, has 25.021 − 24.980 = 0.041 mm of clearance; the tightest pair has 25.000 − 24.993 = 0.007 mm. Always a gap: a sliding fit. These are the same numbers as the preferred-fit tables of ANSI B4.2; this page's unit tests compare all 280 of those tabulated fits (28 sizes, 10 fits) with its own calculation.

Why tight tolerances are invisible at 1:1

On a 24-inch 1920 × 1080 monitor one pixel is about 0.277 mm across. The whole 21 µm tolerance of a 25 mm H7 hole is 0.08 of one pixel, and the largest gap in 25 H7/g6 is 0.15 of a pixel. A typical human hair, about 70 µm thick, is about a quarter of a pixel. So in the true-size drawing the hole and the shaft are the same circle, which is exactly how they look in your hand: a sliding fit and a press fit are indistinguishable by eye.

Workshops measure these sizes with instruments made for micrometre work: outside micrometers and bore gauges, comparators set against gauge blocks, air gauges and coordinate measuring machines. On the production line, go/no-go gauges do most of the work: a plug gauge made at the hole's smallest limit must go in, one made at its largest limit must not.

What is approximate

Sizes in ISO 286 hold at the standard reference temperature of 20 °C (ISO 1). A part that is warmer or colder is a different size, and over tens of micrometres that matters. The drawing treats every surface as a perfect cylinder: real parts are also slightly out of round, not perfectly straight and rough on a small scale, and those form errors are specified separately; they are ignored here. The magnified strips show diameters, as if the shaft rested against the far side of the hole so that all of the clearance appears on one side; when the shaft is centred, the gap on each side is half that. The js values here are ±IT/2 exactly, as in the RoyMech (ISO 286-2) and Miki Pulley (JIS B 0401) tables; the 1988 edition of ISO 286-1 rounded odd IT values in grades 7 to 11 down to the even value below, and some published tables, such as Mitsubishi's, still do.
