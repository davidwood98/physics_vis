Compact objects and the Schwarzschild radius

Hold the mass fixed and shrink the radius. The Sun, 1.39 million km across, becomes a white dwarf about the size of Earth, then a neutron star about 23 km across, then a black hole 5.9 km across. All of Earth squeezed that far would be a horizon 17.7 mm across, drawn here at true size.

The maths

Masses enter as GM (measured far more precisely than G or M alone). c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly, G = 6.6743 × 10−11 m3 kg−1 s−2. Your current values plugged in.

Same mass, smaller radius

What changes, and what doesn't

Squeeze the Sun and its mass, 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg, stays the same. Far away nothing changes: gravity outside a round body depends only on its mass and your distance from its centre, so the planets would keep their orbits. Close to the surface everything changes. Halve the radius and the surface gravity goes up four times, the density eight times. The Sun's mean density is 1.41 g/cm³, a teaspoon weighing 7 g. Squeezed to the size of the white dwarf Sirius B (radius 5,634 km, a little smaller than Earth) a teaspoon weighs 13 tonnes; to neutron-star size, 1.6 billion tonnes.

Why white dwarfs and neutron stars stop shrinking

A normal star is held up by the pressure of its hot gas. When the fuel runs out, something else has to hold it up. In a white dwarf it is the electrons: no two electrons can share the same quantum state, so squeezing them closer forces them into higher and higher energies, and that resists further squeezing (electron degeneracy pressure). Above a limiting mass even that gives way, the electrons are pushed into the nuclei and the star collapses until neutrons, packed as tightly as in an atomic nucleus, hold it up instead: a neutron star. Heavier still, and nothing known can stop the collapse.

The horizon: where escape speed reaches light speed

The Newtonian escape speed from a surface of radius R is √(2GM / R). Set it equal to the speed of light and solve for R: Rs = 2GM / c2. For one solar mass that is 2.95 km. This Newtonian argument happens to give exactly the radius that general relativity gives for the event horizon of a non-spinning black hole, although the meaning is different: in relativity light doesn't slow down and fall back, it simply cannot get out from inside the horizon. The compactness 2GM / (Rc2) measures how close anything is to that: 0.0000042 for the Sun, 0.26 for a neutron star of the Sun's mass, and 1 at the horizon.

Earth's horizon would fit on a coin

Earth's mass parameter gives Rs = 8.87 mm: all 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg of the planet inside a horizon 17.7 mm across, about the size of a UK 5p coin (18.0 mm). Your own mass (70 kg) would make one about 2 × 10⁻²⁵ m across, 8 billion times smaller than a proton.

Sagittarius A*

The stars orbiting the centre of the Milky Way weigh its black hole at 4.297 million solar masses. Its horizon radius is 12.7 million km or 0.0848 AU, about 18 times the Sun's radius, and well inside Mercury's orbit, which never comes closer to the Sun than 0.31 AU. The Event Horizon Telescope image shows a dark shadow about 2.4 times wider than the horizon, because light passing close to the hole is bent and captured.

Worked example: the Sun

Rs = 2 × 1.3271244 × 1020 / 299,792,4582 = 2,953 m. The Sun's real radius, 695,700 km, is 235,600 times bigger. Its escape speed is 618 km/s, about 0.21% of the speed of light.

What is approximate

Density, surface gravity and escape speed are Newtonian. Near a neutron star relativity already matters, and at a horizon the Newtonian surface gravity has no meaning. Real black holes spin, which makes the horizon smaller than 2GM / c2; the page uses the non-spinning case. The white dwarf and neutron-star stages use measured radii of real stars whose masses differ from the Sun's: Sirius B has 1.018 solar masses and PSR J0437−4715 1.418, and radii have uncertainties (the neutron star's is +0.95 / −0.63 km). NICER's other measured neutron star, PSR J0030+0451, comes out at 12.7 to 13.0 km. The Sun itself will never become a neutron star or a black hole: it is not heavy enough.
