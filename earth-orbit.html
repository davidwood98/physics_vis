Earth is drawn exactly 2 cm across, as at the closest stop of the space page. At that scale the edge of space is thinner than a pixel and the International Space Station skims 0.63 mm above the surface. What makes orbit hard is speed, not height.

Low orbit is low. It is the speed that is hard.

At 2 cm, low Earth orbit hugs the surface

Draw Earth 2 cm across and each kilometre shrinks to 0.0016 mm. The Kármán line, the 100 km height the FAI uses as the boundary of space, sits 0.16 mm above the surface: thinner than one pixel on a 24-inch 1080p monitor, whose pixels are 0.277 mm wide. The International Space Station orbits at about 250 miles (402 km), just 0.63 mm up. GPS satellites are 3.2 cm out, geostationary satellites 5.6 cm, and the Moon 60 cm from Earth's centre, off the edge of most screens.

What makes orbit hard is speed

An orbiting spacecraft is falling all the time. It stays up because it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it as quickly as it falls. At the ISS's height that takes 7.67 km/s, a lap every 92 minutes. Lifting each kilogram up to that height takes 3.7 MJ of energy; getting it up to orbital speed takes 29.4 MJ. So only about 11% of the energy goes into height and 89% into speed. A rocket that went straight up to 400 km and stopped would simply fall back down.

Orbital speed and escape speed

The speed for a circular orbit, v = √(GM / r), falls the further out you go. The escape speed, enough to leave for good, is √(2GM / r): always √2 (about 1.41) times the orbital speed at the same distance, so a circular orbit always runs at about 71% of the local escape speed. From the surface, escape takes 11.2 km/s. At the ISS's height it is 10.8 km/s, barely less: 400 km is a small step away from a planet 6,371 km in radius.

Why geostationary orbit is 35,786 km up

The further out, the slower the lap. A satellite that goes round once per sidereal day (one turn of the Earth, 23 h 56 min 4 s) keeps pace with the ground and seems to hang still in the sky. Kepler's third law gives the radius: r = ∛(GM T2 / 4π2) = 42,164 km from Earth's centre, which is 35,786 km above the equator, moving at only 3.07 km/s. In the animation the marked spot on Earth's equator and the geostationary satellite turn together.

Worked example: the ISS

The orbit radius is 6,371 + 402 km = 6,773 km. The speed is √(3.986 × 1014 / 6.773 × 10⁶) = 7,671 m/s, and one lap of 2π × 6,773 km takes 5,548 s, about 92 minutes. NASA rounds this to "every 90 minutes". On the 2 cm Earth the station is 0.63 mm up and moves at 0.012 mm/s: in real time it takes 5.8 s to creep the width of a human hair.

What is approximate

Orbits are drawn as circles in one plane, seen from above the North Pole. The real ISS and GPS orbits are tilted to the equator and slightly oval. The ISS's height also wanders: air drag slowly lowers it and engine burns raise it again. NASA describes it as about 250 miles up; its trajectory data for October 2026 puts it at 417 to 424 km. Earth is drawn as a sphere with the mean radius, and heights are measured above that sphere, except the geostationary height, which is quoted above the equator (the two differ by 7 km). The energy split ignores air drag, the energy lost fighting gravity during the climb, and the head start that Earth's spin gives a rocket launched eastwards, so real rockets need more than this. Satellites are drawn as dots; at this scale every real one is far smaller than a pixel.
