Acceleration, explained

What acceleration means

Acceleration is how fast velocity changes: a = Δv / Δt, in metres per second per second (m/s2). At a constant acceleration the speed grows steadily, v = u + a t, but the distance grows with the square of time, x = u t + ½ a t2. That's why the marks the dot leaves at equal time steps get further and further apart.

0 to 60 mph

60 mph is 26.8 m/s, so a car that does 0–60 in 6 s averages 26.8 / 6 ≈ 4.5 m/s2, a little under half of 1 g. Even so, from rest it crosses a 30 cm screen in about 0.37 s: at true scale, real accelerations are over almost before you see them.

Braking and overshoot

Braking is acceleration against the motion. The stopping distance is d = u2 / 2a, so it grows with the square of speed: double the speed and you need four times the distance. The UK Highway Code gives a braking distance of 14 m from 30 mph, which works out to about 6.4 m/s2. If the stopping distance is longer than your screen, the dot runs off the edge and the page tells you how far beyond it would stop.

Free fall and 1 g

Near the Earth's surface everything falls with the same acceleration, g = 9.81 m/s2 (1 g), if air resistance is negligible. Falling a height h takes t = √(2h / g): about 0.45 s for 1 m. On the Moon, where gravity is about one sixth as strong, the same drop takes √6 ≈ 2.4 times longer.
