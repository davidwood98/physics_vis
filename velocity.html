Linear and angular velocity, explained

Linear velocity

Velocity is how fast position changes. At a constant velocity v, position grows steadily with time: x = x0 + v t. At 2 m/s you cover 2 metres every second, so a 1 m stretch of ruler takes 0.5 s. Speeds are often quoted in other units: 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h ≈ 2.24 mph.

Angular velocity

For something turning, we track the angle instead: θ = θ0 + ω t, where the angular velocity ω is in radians per second. One full turn is 2π radians, so the frequency is f = ω / 2π turns per second, the period is T = 1 / f and revolutions per minute are rpm = 60 f.

Linking the two: v = ω r

A point on a circle of radius r moves along the rim at v = ω r. At the same ω, double the radius and the point moves twice as fast, which is why the tip of a fan blade outruns its hub. The speed is constant but the direction keeps changing, so the point accelerates towards the centre: the centripetal acceleration a = v2 / r = ω2 r.

Worked example

A point on a 0.1 m radius moving at 2 m/s has ω = 2 / 0.1 = 20 rad/s. That is 20 / 2π ≈ 3.18 turns per second, or about 191 rpm, with a centripetal acceleration of 22 / 0.1 = 40 m/s2, about four times gravity.

Why 1:1 scale needs calibration

Browsers measure in CSS pixels, and a CSS pixel is a different physical size on every screen. Until you calibrate, the page assumes 96 pixels per inch, which can be out by 50% or more on laptops and phones. Matching a bank card (85.60 mm wide on every card worldwide) tells the page how many pixels make a millimetre on your screen, so 1 m on the ruler is 1 m in real life. Browser zoom is accounted for automatically; a different monitor needs a new calibration.
