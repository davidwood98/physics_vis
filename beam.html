Beam deflection, explained

Stiffness is E times I

How far a beam bends depends on two numbers multiplied together. E, the modulus of elasticity, belongs to the material: steel's is about three times aluminium's and about 19 times that of softwood along the grain. I, the second moment of area, belongs to the shape of the cross-section. Every deflection formula divides by EI, so doubling either one halves the bend.

Why depth matters so much

For a rectangle I = b h3 / 12: the depth is cubed. Turn the 250 × 18 mm shelf board on its edge and it becomes 193 times stiffer, with exactly the same wood. That is why joists stand on edge and why I-beams put their material in flanges far from the middle. Span matters even more: a point load's deflection grows with L3, so a shelf twice as long sags eight times as far.

The 1:1 lesson

Load an IPE 100 steel beam spanning 1 m with 4 tonnes at mid-span and the bending stress reaches about 85% of the yield strength. It sags only about 2.5 mm: around 9 pixels on a 24-inch 1080p monitor. A beam close to yielding may move only a millimetre or two. Most of the sagging you notice in real life comes from much longer spans or heavier loads, or from creep: timber and plastics keep bending slowly under a load left for months. The acrylic maker, for example, gives a short-term tensile strength of 80 MPa but a maximum long-term stress of only 5 to 10 MPa.

Worked example

A 1 m pine shelf board, 250 mm deep and 18 mm thick, rests on supports at its ends with 20 kg of books in the middle. The load is P = 20 × 9.80665 = 196 N. The section has I = 250 × 183 / 12 = 121,500 mm4, and C24 softwood has E = 11 GPa. So δ = P L3 / 48 E I = 3.06 mm, about L/327. The moment is P L / 4 = 49 N·m and the stress M c / I = 3.63 MPa, about 15% of the timber's characteristic bending strength. Halve the span to 500 mm and the sag falls to 0.38 mm.

What is approximate

The formulas are the standard Euler–Bernoulli ones: small deflections, a linear elastic material, plane sections staying plane, no shear deformation, and ideal supports (a perfectly rigid wall, frictionless pins and rollers). They ignore creep, temperature, holes, joints and connections, local crushing under the load, and every kind of buckling. Timber values are for a strength class, not your piece of wood, and a furniture board is not strength graded at all. Material values are typical or minimum figures from datasheets. The simplified I-section has no root fillets, so it gives 163 cm4 for an IPE 100 against the tabulated 171 cm4. The page warns when the deflection passes a tenth of the span (the formulas assume small deflections) or when the stress passes the material's strength.
