How a suction cup holds, and how much it can hold

Vacuum doesn't pull: the air pushes

A suction cup has no grip of its own. Pumping air out of the cup lowers the pressure inside, and the atmosphere outside, about 101 kPa at sea level, presses the product against the cup. The holding force is the pressure difference times the area it acts on: F = Δp × A. A gauge reading of −60 kPa means the cup is 60 kPa below the outside air, which is 59% of a perfect vacuum. Even a perfect vacuum could only ever give 101 kPa of pressure difference.

Area matters more than vacuum

The force grows with the area, so with the square of the diameter: double the diameter and the force is four times as big, while doubling the vacuum only doubles it. A deeper vacuum also wears cups faster and takes longer to build up, so the usual fix for a weak gripper is bigger or more cups. The area is the one that is really sealed: a real cup holds less than its nominal diameter suggests. Schmalz's SUF 90 cup, for example, lists 328 N at −60 kPa, where the full 90 mm would give 382 N: it behaves like an 83 mm cup.

Three load cases

I: cup horizontal, lifting. The cups carry the weight plus the force to accelerate it upwards, m (g + a). II: cup horizontal, moving sideways. The cups still carry the weight, but the sideways push has to come through friction between the cup lip and the product. Friction is at most μ times the force pressing them together, so producing a sideways force m a needs an extra m a / μ of holding force. III: cup vertical. The cup is on a vertical face, so the whole weight hangs on friction: the cups need (m / μ)(g + a).

Why sideways moves are expensive

With a dry friction coefficient of 0.5, every 1 m/s2 of sideways acceleration needs as much extra holding force as 2 m/s2 of upward acceleration. On an oily surface, where μ can be as low as 0.1, it needs as much as 10 m/s2 of lift. That is why products slide off grippers that lift them perfectly well, and why emergency stops, which brake the hardest, are the case to check.

Why safety factors differ between manufacturers

Schmalz puts the acceleration and the friction into the formula and then multiplies by a safety factor of at least 1.5 for smooth, dense workpieces, 2 or more for porous, rough or oiled ones, and 2.5 or more if the workpiece is swivelled. SMC works from the static weight alone and asks for a theoretical lifting force 4 times the weight when the pad is horizontal and 8 times when it is vertical. SMC points out that its theoretical force is a static value, so the safety factor has to allow for the real operating conditions such as acceleration, wind pressure and impacts, and it advises keeping sideways acceleration as low as possible. Neither is wrong: they put the margin in different places. SMC's rule is simpler and more cautious at low acceleration; Schmalz's grows with the acceleration and friction you enter. With μ = 0.5 and S = 1.5, Schmalz asks for more than SMC once a sideways move accelerates faster than 12.3 m/s2.

Worked example

Schmalz's own example lifts a 2.5 m × 1.25 m × 2.5 mm steel sheet (61.33 kg) and moves it sideways at 5 m/s2 with μ = 0.5 and S = 1.5: 61.33 × (9.81 + 5 / 0.5) × 1.5 = 1,822 N. Shared over six cups that is 304 N each, which at −60 kPa needs an effective diameter of 80 mm; Schmalz picks six SUF 90 cups. SMC's rule for the same sheet asks for 4 × 61.33 × 9.81 = 2,407 N, 401 N per cup, which needs 92 mm: SMC's next size up is ø100. Load the "Steel sheet" example to try it.

What is simplified

The holding force here is the theoretical, static value for a perfectly sealed cup on a flat, rigid product. Leaks, porous materials such as cardboard, flexible products that peel away, worn or deformed cups, the time to build up vacuum, the moment from an off-centre load and air resistance on large sheets all reduce what a real gripper can hold. The product is drawn as a plain box and its size does not change the forces; only its mass does.
