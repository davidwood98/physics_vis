The scale is set by your screen: Neptune's whole orbit is fitted to the canvas, so the bigger the canvas the bigger everything is drawn. Use Full screen and the solar system spans your whole monitor (1 px = – on this screen). Scroll, pinch or use +/− to zoom; click a planet or moon to make it the zoom focus (the Sun otherwise). Earth is exactly one pixel. Drag or use the arrow keys to pan; the arrow at the edge points to the Sun. Fly to the Sun or Mars, or send a light pulse and follow it. A guided journey: pick a place to visit, or step between places with +/−, the arrow keys or the scroll wheel. The Milky Way and galaxies are static at these scales: they barely move in a human lifetime. Press Space to play or pause. Hover anything for how far away it would be from your screen, or click a label for a fact card. Background stars are decorative.

      How big is space on your screen?

      The Sun is a fraction of a pixel

      Squeeze Neptune's whole orbit onto your screen and each pixel has to cover millions of kilometres (the exact figure depends on your canvas: in full screen it is –). The Sun is 1.39 million km across, so it shrinks to about a fifth of a pixel, and Earth to about a thousandth. The planets you usually see in diagrams are drawn hundreds of times too big. Only the orbits are to scale. Zoom in on any planet to see its moons: all 454 moons of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune in JPL's list are drawn, most of them small captured rocks with no measured size.

      Earth as one pixel

      Make Earth exactly one pixel and the Sun becomes a disc about 109 pixels wide, 11,741 pixels away: over 6 widths of a 1920-pixel screen. The Moon sits about 30 pixels from Earth. Light from the Sun takes about 499 seconds to cross that gap, and Earth itself, racing along its orbit at about 29.78 km/s, creeps across your screen at only 0.0023 pixels per second.

      The galactic scale, and the Milky Way as one pixel

      Shrink our galaxy, about 100,000 light-years across, to a single pixel and the Sun sits about 0.27 pixels from its centre. Andromeda, about 2.5 million light-years away, is roughly 25 pixels away: 7 mm on a 24-inch 1080p monitor. Most of the screen is empty of drawn objects. That is because the galaxy catalogue stops at 359 pixels (11 megaparsecs), not because space does: beyond it there are countless more galaxies that simply aren't drawn here. The journey stops at places along the way; galaxies big enough to see are drawn as artist's impressions of their catalogued type, and the Milky Way picture follows the measured spiral arms of Reid et al. (2019) and the bar of Wegg et al. (2015).

      Markers or true size?

      In Markers mode, anything smaller than a few pixels gets a labelled ring so you can find it. True size draws everything at its real size; anything under a pixel becomes a single pixel whose brightness is the fraction of the pixel it would cover, so the Sun in the solar-system view is a barely visible dot.

      What is approximate

      Planet positions come from approximate J2000 orbital elements and are drawn flat (inclinations ignored), so they can be off by a few degrees; they are for visualisation only. The Moon is on a circular orbit timed from the mean new moon of 6 January 2000. Moon orbits are flat versions of JPL's mean orbits. The Milky Way picture uses a measured arm model but its colours are artistic, galaxy pictures are impressions, the background stars are decorative, and distances beyond about 100 million light-years come from redshifts.
