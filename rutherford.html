How a gold foil revealed the nucleus

What was expected, and what was seen

Before 1911 the leading picture of the atom was J. J. Thomson's "plum pudding": electrons dotted through a ball of positive charge the size of the whole atom. Spread out like that, the charge pushes only gently. Allowing for the positive charge alone, one gold atom (radius about 0.144 nm) can turn a 5 MeV alpha by at most about 0.02°, and 3,500 such nudges in random directions add up to at most about 1°. Turning one round through 90° or more should essentially never happen.

Geiger and Marsden found that it does. Alphas fired at a metal plate sometimes came back off the side they went in. From a platinum plate, they reported that about 1 in 8,000 came back. Rutherford's explanation in 1911 was that the positive charge sits in a tiny nucleus at the centre, which also carries almost all the mass (a gold atom's electrons add up to only 0.02% of it). An alpha that happens to pass very close to it feels a huge push and can be turned right round. In 1913 Geiger and Marsden counted over 100,000 flashes of scattered alphas in all. With gold and silver foils, at angles from 5° to 150°, the numbers fell as 1/sin4(θ/2), just as Rutherford predicted.

Why most alphas go straight through

An atom is almost all empty space as far as an alpha is concerned. The gold nucleus is about 7 fm (femtometres, 10−15 m) in radius, while the atoms are 0.288 nm apart, so the nucleus fills about one part in 12 trillion of the space each atom has. At 1 cm per atom, as in the drawing, the nucleus would be 0.5 µm across: far smaller than one pixel. The electrons are too light to turn an alpha by much. So an alpha usually passes every nucleus at a respectful distance and is nudged by a degree or so at most.

Turned back 45 fm out

Fire an alpha straight at a nucleus and it slows as the electric repulsion builds, stops, and comes back. It stops where all its kinetic energy has become electrical potential energy, the distance d = zZe2/(4πε0E). For a 5 MeV alpha (charge z = 2) and gold (Z = 79) that is 45.5 fm if the nucleus stays put, or 46.4 fm allowing for the small recoil of the gold nucleus. The nucleus' edge is at about 7.0 fm (a uniform sphere with the measured rms charge radius of 5.44 fm), so the alpha is turned back 6.6 times further out than the edge. It is repelled before it ever touches, which is why Rutherford's point-charge formula works so well for gold.

Alphas that miss by an impact parameter b follow a hyperbola and are turned through θ = 2 arctan(d/2b). Aim at b = d/2, about 23 fm, and the alpha turns through 90°. Miss by just one picometre, under 1% of the gap between atoms, and it turns by only about 2.7°.

The cross-section

To turn by more than an angle θ, an alpha has to arrive within b(θ) = (d/2) cot(θ/2) of a nucleus: inside a small target disc of area σ(>θ) = π(d/2)2cot2(θ/2) around it. That area is the cross-section. A thin foil holds n t nuclei per square metre (n atoms per cubic metre, thickness t), so the fraction of the beam that hits one of those discs is P = n t σ, as long as the discs hardly overlap.

Worked example

Gold has 5.91 × 1028 atoms per cubic metre (from its density and molar mass), so a 1 µm foil holds 5.91 × 1022 per square metre. For 5 MeV alphas the cross-section for turning beyond 90° is 1,630 fm2 (1.63 × 10−27 m2). Multiply: 9.6 × 10−5, or about 1 in 10,400. The same sum for 10° gives about 1 in 80. Press Fire 1,000,000 and the Monte Carlo count lands close to these.

How the historical 1 in 8,000 relates

The famous figure is not a gold-foil number, and the page does not reuse it. Geiger and Marsden's 1909 measurement used a thick platinum plate and the alphas of "radium C" (now known to come from polonium-214, 7.69 MeV), which first crossed a little over a centimetre of air (down to about 7.0 MeV, from NIST's ASTAR ranges). The plate was thick, but the returning alphas came from a thin surface layer: about half of them from a layer equivalent to about 2 mm of air, roughly 0.6 µm of metal by their estimate. So the layer that counts is of the order of a micrometre. One micrometre of platinum at 7.0 MeV gives about 1 in 18,600 beyond 90° (the 1909 preset): the same order as 1 in 8,000. The rest of the gap is the set-up. The alphas struck the plate at many angles, and one arriving at a slant can leave the front face after turning less than 90°. Alphas also slow down inside the plate, and slower alphas scatter more. And the total was scaled up from counts at a few places by assuming the returning alphas spread evenly over a hemisphere. Rutherford himself called the experiment "not very suited for accurate calculation", and from it estimated a central charge of about 100 electron charges for platinum, against the 78 we now know.

What is approximate

Rutherford's formula treats the alpha and the nucleus as point charges that only repel, with one close encounter per alpha and slow (non-relativistic) motion. A 5 MeV alpha moves at about 5.2% of light speed, so that last part is fine. The point-charge part fails once alphas can reach the nucleus' surface: for gold at about 25 MeV, for aluminium at about 7 MeV, where the nuclear force adds its own pull. The atom's electrons partly shield the nucleus from alphas passing far out, so the smallest angles are slightly overstated. Real foils also slow the alphas (a 5 MeV alpha loses about 0.46 MeV crossing 1 µm of gold) and add many small random turns (multiple scattering), which blur the small-angle end of the histogram. The drawing shows a slice through the crystal; every alpha is drawn turning in the plane of the screen, and its motion is slowed by about 1015 times so you can follow it.
