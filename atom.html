How big is an atom, really?

Atom = 1 cm

Make a hydrogen atom 1 cm across and you have magnified it about 32 million times. A centimetre is about 36 pixels on a 24-inch 1080p monitor. Its nucleus, a single proton, is then only 0.055 µm across, and a gold nucleus in a 1 cm gold atom is 0.24 µm: about 1/1,150 of a pixel. No screen can draw that, so the insets magnify the centre a hundred times, and a hundred times again, until the nucleus shows up.

Where is the edge of an atom?

An atom has no hard surface. Its electrons are not dots on orbits but a cloud of probability that thins out smoothly with distance. To give every atom a size we use one definition throughout: the distance at which the electron density of a free atom falls to 0.001 electrons per cubic bohr (Rahm, Hoffmann and Ashcroft, 2016). For hydrogen the exact 1s cloud reaches that density at 152 pm, close to their 154 pm, and 93% of the electron lies inside. That edge is what we draw 1 cm across, and the cloud fades on beyond it. Other definitions give other sizes: the Bohr radius, where hydrogen's electron is most likely to be found, is 52.9 pm, about a third as big.

With this definition atoms barely change size across the periodic table: their radius runs from helium (134 pm) to barium (293 pm), a factor of only 2.2. Nuclei grow steadily instead: a nucleus's volume is roughly proportional to its mass number A, so its radius grows as A1/3, from 1.68 fm for hydrogen to 11.7 fm for uranium. The chart shows both on the same logarithmic scale.

Mostly empty, but only by volume

The nucleus fills between one part in 6 × 1015 and one part in 7 × 1013 of an atom's volume, yet holds more than 99.9% of its mass. The rest of the atom is not empty in the everyday sense: it is filled by the electron cloud and its electric field. Matter feels solid because the electron clouds of neighbouring atoms repel each other, and the Pauli exclusion principle stops their electrons from crowding into the same states.

In a solid, the size that counts is the spacing

Chemists talk about valence bands and energy levels, but those are energies, not distances, so they cannot be drawn to scale. What can be drawn 1:1 in a solid is the lattice: in copper metal, neighbouring atoms are 256 pm apart, closer than a free copper atom is wide, so their clouds overlap. Turn on In a solid to see the measured spacing at the same scale.

Proton = 1 cm

Now magnify a proton until it is 1 cm across, about 6 trillion times. A hydrogen atom would be 1.83 km across and a gold atom 2.69 km. (With the Bohr radius instead of the density edge, hydrogen would be 629 m.) Quarks have no measured size: experiments only set an upper limit on their radius of 4.3 × 10−19 m, so on a 1 cm proton a quark is under 5.1 µm across, under 0.02 of a pixel, and may be a true point. Gluons carry the strong force: they are quanta of a field, with no size to draw.

So the proton is drawn as a fuzzy region filled with a restless sea of quark and antiquark pairs and gluons, not as three balls. The three valence quarks (two up, one down) give the proton its charge, but their masses add up to only 9.02 MeV/c2, 0.96% of its 938.27 MeV/c2. The other 99% is the energy of the moving quarks and the gluon field, through E = mc2.

Worked example: gold

A free gold atom is 452 pm across, so drawing it 1 cm wide magnifies it 22 million times. Its gold-197 nucleus is 10.9 fm across (twice its rms charge radius of 5.44 fm), so on screen it is 0.24 µm. A pixel on a 24-inch 1080p monitor is 277 µm, so the nucleus is about 1/1,150 of a pixel.

What is approximate

Sizes depend on definitions: other atomic radii (covalent, metallic, van der Waals) differ by up to a factor of three, and a nucleus drawn as a uniform ball with the same rms radius would be 1.29 times wider. The cloud of hydrogen is the exact 1s density; other atoms are drawn as smooth clouds with the same edge, without their inner shells. Brightness is compressed so the faint outer cloud shows. The quark sea is an illustration of a quantum field: its pairs, gluons and motion are not to scale in size, number or time. Quark masses are the Particle Data Group's values at a scale of 2 GeV; a quark's mass depends on how it is defined, but every definition leaves the valence quarks a small part of the proton's mass.
