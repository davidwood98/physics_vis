Why short moves never reach top speed

The speed limit is a ceiling, not a target

A point-to-point move starts and ends at rest. The axis speeds up at its acceleration limit, and it has to start braking early enough to stop on the target. Speeding up to v takes v2 / 2a of distance, and braking takes the same again, so a trapezoidal move only reaches its speed limit if it is longer than v2 / a. At 2 m/s and 10 m/s2 (about 1 g) that is 0.4 m. Anything shorter is a triangle: speed up, then straight away slow down, peaking at √(a d).

So acceleration sets the cycle time

On a short move the time is 2 √(d / a), which does not contain the speed limit at all. Doubling the speed limit saves nothing; doubling the acceleration cuts the move time by about 29%. This is the commonest mistake in cycle-time estimates: dividing the distance by the top speed. That gives the right answer only for long moves. For a pick-and-place robot doing short hops, the acceleration (and, for S-curves, the jerk) is what you are really buying.

Trapezoid or S-curve?

A trapezoidal profile switches acceleration on and off instantly, so its jerk (the rate of change of acceleration) is infinite at every corner. Real mechanics can't follow that: the sudden change in force excites vibration in the arm, belts and gripper, and the higher the jerk, the more vibration energy and the more vibration modes are excited. An S-curve ramps the acceleration up and down at a limited jerk, giving seven phases instead of three. It is smoother, but it is never quicker: each ramp loses some time. The trade-off pays off when the vibration it avoids would otherwise add settling time at the place position.

Worked example

A 50 mm move with limits of 2 m/s, 10 m/s2 and 500 m/s3. The trapezoid never gets near 2 m/s: it peaks at √(10 × 0.05) = 0.71 m/s and takes 141 ms. The S-curve spends 20 ms ramping the acceleration up and down each time, peaks at 0.61 m/s and takes 163 ms. With 0.15 s dwells at each end, a cycle is 0.583 s (103 picks per minute) for the trapezoid and 0.626 s (96 per minute) for the S-curve. Doubling the S-curve's speed limit saves nothing; doubling its acceleration saves 15 ms per move and doubling its jerk saves 11 ms.

Check it against your own machine

Robot and drive data sheets give the speed, acceleration and often the jerk limits. Type them in with your measured pick and place times and compare the picks per minute with what the machine really does. If the real machine is slower, the difference is usually the things this model leaves out.

What is simplified

Every move here is a single axis moving from rest to rest along a straight line. Real robots coordinate several axes (the slowest one sets the pace), may blend moves together without stopping, and need settling time at the end of each move while vibration dies away. Motor torque limits can also mean the acceleration available falls off at higher speeds. The dwell times stand in for gripping, vacuum build-up and release.
