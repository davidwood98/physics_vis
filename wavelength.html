    Wavelengths, explained

    λ = v / f

    A wave repeats in space as well as in time. In one period T = 1 / f the wave moves on by exactly one wavelength, so λ = v T = v / f. The same frequency gives very different lengths in different materials: sound in air moves at about 343 m/s at 20 °C, sound in water at about 1,482 m/s, and radio waves and light at 299,792,458 m/s.

    Worked example: the note A that orchestras tune to is 440 Hz (ISO 16). In dry air at 20 °C, 343.4 / 440 = 0.780 m, so one wavelength of it is about 78 cm. The deepest note most people hear, 20 Hz, is about 17 m long, about 0.72 × the length of a tennis court.

    Why ultrasonic sensors are small

    A 40 kHz ultrasonic distance sensor sends sound with a wavelength of only 8.6 mm. To send sound out as a beam, rather than spraying it in every direction, a source has to be at least about a wavelength across, and the same goes for catching the echo. At 40 kHz that is under a centimetre, so a transducer can of 12.5 mm (about 1.5 wavelengths) is enough: its datasheet gives a beam about 75° wide. A loudspeaker that beamed 440 Hz as tightly would need to be the same number of wavelengths across, about 1.1 m, which is why ordinary speakers send low notes all round a room.

    Why Wi-Fi antennas are a few centimetres

    Simple antennas work best when they are a half or a quarter of a wavelength long. The 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi band (2.4 to 2.4835 GHz) has wavelengths of 12.1 to 12.5 cm, so a quarter wave is about 3.1 cm and a half wave about 6.1 cm: that is why Wi-Fi antennas are only a few centimetres long. Microwave ovens use 2.45 GHz, about 12.2 cm, almost the same. The 5 GHz Wi-Fi bands (5.15 to 5.85 GHz) are about half that: 5.1 to 5.8 cm. FM radio at 100 MHz is 3 m long, so a quarter-wave FM aerial is about 75 cm.

    Light is smaller than a pixel

    Visible light runs from about 380 to 700 nm (NASA). One pixel on a 24-inch 1920 × 1080 monitor is 0.277 mm across, so red light at 700 nm is about 395 times smaller than a pixel and violet at 400 nm about 692 times smaller. A green laser pointer (532 nm) fits about 520 wavelengths across one pixel. No screen can draw a wave of light at true size, so the Wavelength view switches to a magnified pixel when the wavelength gets below about two pixels.

    Why you can see through a microwave door

    The window in a microwave oven door is covered by a metal sheet full of round holes, typically about 1.5 mm across. Each hole behaves like a very short metal pipe. A round metal pipe of diameter d only carries waves shorter than its cut-off wavelength, λc ≈ 1.706 d (the TE11 mode). For a 1.5 mm hole that is 2.56 mm; for a 2 mm hole 3.41 mm. The oven's 12.2 cm microwaves are about 48 times too long to get through.

    A wave below cut-off does not stop dead at the hole: it fades exponentially inside it. For wavelengths much longer than the cut-off the field falls by a factor of e every λc / 2π, which is about 32 dB of power for every hole diameter of depth. In a 1.5 mm hole that is about 21 dB per millimetre. Even a hole in a thin sheet lets through very little, because it is so much smaller than the wavelength. Light, at around 0.0005 mm, is thousands of times shorter than the cut-off (a 1.5 mm hole is about 2,820 green wavelengths across), so it passes straight through and you can watch your food. A hole wider than about 7.2 cm would let the oven's microwaves through.

    Standing waves, room modes and cavity resonances

    Between two hard walls a distance L apart, a wave that bounces back and forth only builds up when a whole number of half-wavelengths fit: L = n λ / 2, so fn = n v / (2L). In a 4 m room the first of these room modes is at 42.9 Hz, then 85.8, 129 Hz and so on. At those bass notes the room booms in some places and goes quiet in others; sound pressure is highest at the walls and in the corners. A real room has three pairs of walls, so it has modes from each length plus combinations of them, and a smaller room has its modes at higher frequencies, spaced further apart. That is why room acoustics depends on size.

    A microwave oven is a metal box, and the same rule applies along each of its sides. Across a 290 mm wide cavity the modes are 517 MHz apart, and the oven's 2.45 GHz sits nearest mode 5. The field has hot spots about half a wavelength apart, roughly 6 cm, which is why ovens have a turntable to move the food through them.

    What is approximate

    The speed of sound in air uses Cramer's equation as coded by NPL, valid from 0 to 30 °C at normal pressure; water uses Marczak's equation for pure water, valid from 0 to 95 °C. Sea water and real rooms with soft furnishings behave differently. Radio and light use the speed in a vacuum: air slows light by under 0.03% (refractive index 1.00027, NIST), far too little to show. The cut-off model treats each hole as an ideal round metal pipe; real meshes are close to this but not exact. The standing-wave view is one-dimensional: real rooms and cavities have modes in all three directions. Hole sizes vary between ovens, and the pixel comparisons are only as good as your screen calibration.
