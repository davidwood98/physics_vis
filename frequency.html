    Frequency and vibration, explained

    Frequency and period

    Frequency is how many times something repeats each second, measured in hertz (Hz). The period is the time for one repeat: T = 1 / f. At 1 Hz a cycle takes a second; at 50 Hz it takes 20 milliseconds, faster than you can follow by eye. Most screens refresh 60 times a second, so they can't show anything faster than 30 Hz as separate frames.

    Small amplitude, big acceleration

    A vibration that moves x = A sin(2πft) reaches a peak speed of 2πfA and a peak acceleration of (2πf)2A. Acceleration grows with the square of frequency. A 1 mm vibration at 50 Hz is barely visible at true scale, yet its peak acceleration is about 99 m/s2, around 10 g. Its RMS velocity of over 200 mm/s is far above the few mm/s that machinery standards treat as healthy for a typical machine.

    Why it damages bearings

    Force is mass times acceleration, so 10 g means every part is pushed back and forth with ten times its own weight. At 50 Hz it does this 180,000 times an hour, over 4 million times a day. Bearings, welds and bolts fail by fatigue under that many cycles, even when each one looks tiny.

    Towards sound

    Above about 20 Hz, vibrations in the air become sounds we can hear. Mains hum is at 50 or 60 Hz and its harmonics, and the same maths describes a speaker cone moving a fraction of a millimetre.
